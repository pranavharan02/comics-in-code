# Five scripts

Every quote is verified public domain (see `quotes/quotes.json`). None of the five authors has been
adapted by Zen Pencils (all 223 archive pages were checked). Stories, characters and art are original.
Each script names its **colour script**, its **repetition device** and its **turn**, the three things
the archive study found in almost every strong Zen Pencils strip.

---

## 1. UP-HILL (Christina Rossetti, 1862)
The poem is already a conversation, so it becomes one: a small girl asks, her grandad answers,
on a road that climbs one hill from morning to night.

- **Cast:** ISLA (6), red raincoat, yellow wellies, bob hair. GRANDAD (late 70s), tall and stooped,
  flat cap, green jumper, walking stick, white moustache.
- **Colour script:** dawn mint and cream → noon gold → afternoon amber → dusk violet → night indigo,
  with one warm orange window that grows from a dot to a doorway.
- **Repetition:** the same hill in the same framing in panels 1, 3 and 5, as the day darkens and the
  two dots climb it.
- **Turn:** he carries her the last stretch; the door opens on everyone who went before.

| # | shot | words (balloons: Isla asks, Grandad answers) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title. Wide: the whole hill at dawn, a road zig-zagging to a tiny inn at the top; two tiny figures at the gate at the bottom | ISLA: DOES THE ROAD WIND UP-HILL ALL THE WAY? · GRANDAD: YES, TO THE VERY END. |
| 2 | Medium: the two walking, side view, noon; her hand in his | WILL THE DAY'S JOURNEY TAKE THE WHOLE LONG DAY? · FROM MORN TO NIGHT, MY FRIEND. |
| 3 | Same hill as 1, afternoon: the dots halfway up | (none) |
| 4 | They sit on a dry-stone wall sharing a sandwich; long shadows | BUT IS THERE FOR THE NIGHT A RESTING-PLACE? · A ROOF FOR WHEN THE SLOW DARK HOURS BEGIN. |
| 5 | Same hill, dusk violet: dots near the top; one warm window lit | (none) |
| 6 | Close: Isla's worried face, dark trees behind | MAY NOT THE DARKNESS HIDE IT FROM MY FACE? |
| 7 | Grandad crouches, pointing up at the lit window | YOU CANNOT MISS THAT INN. |
| 8 | Night: lantern-carrying silhouettes join the road ahead of them | SHALL I MEET OTHER WAYFARERS AT NIGHT? · THOSE WHO HAVE GONE BEFORE. |
| 9 | Grandad carries sleepy Isla on his back up the last steps | THEN MUST I KNOCK, OR CALL WHEN JUST IN SIGHT? · THEY WILL NOT KEEP YOU STANDING AT THAT DOOR. |
| 10 | The inn door swings open: warm light, kind faces, a hearth | SHALL I FIND COMFORT, TRAVEL-SORE AND WEAK? · OF LABOUR YOU SHALL FIND THE SUM. |
| 11 | Splash: Isla asleep in a big bed under a starry window; Grandad dozing in the chair beside her, cap on his knee | WILL THERE BE BEDS FOR ME AND ALL WHO SEEK? · YEA, BEDS FOR ALL WHO COME. – CHRISTINA ROSSETTI |

## 2. THE GREAT OCEAN (Isaac Newton, as reported in 1855)
A famous physicist leaves her own award dinner for the beach and finds the only person there who
doesn't know who she is.

- **Cast:** DR. ADA OKAFOR (82), short white hair, round glasses, emerald gown. TOBY (8),
  pyjamas, wellies, bucket and torch.
- **Colour script:** hot gold ballroom → cool blue beach → the torch's small warm circle →
  a huge dark sky and sea lit by stars and blue-green glowing waves.
- **Repetition:** hands: hers holding the trophy, then shoes, then a pebble; then her hand next to his.
- **Turn:** scale. The medal-winner becomes a tiny figure at the edge of an enormous ocean.

| # | shot | caption |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title. Ballroom: banner LIFETIME ACHIEVEMENT, tiny Ada on stage holding a gold trophy, applause | I DO NOT KNOW WHAT I MAY APPEAR TO THE WORLD, |
| 2 | Close: her face, polite smile, eyes sliding to the balcony door | (none) |
| 3 | She slips out barefoot, shoes in one hand; the trophy left on a chair | BUT TO MYSELF I SEEM TO HAVE BEEN ONLY LIKE A BOY PLAYING ON THE SEA-SHORE, |
| 4 | The beach at night; far away, a small torch light | (none) |
| 5 | Toby crouched over the sand with his torch; she crouches beside him | AND DIVERTING MYSELF IN NOW AND THEN FINDING A SMOOTHER PEBBLE |
| 6 | Close: her old hand and his small hand, a shell between them in the torch circle | OR A PRETTIER SHELL THAN ORDINARY, |
| 7 | Both look up (small, seen from behind) | (none) |
| 8 | Splash: vast night ocean, glowing waves, tiny figures at the bottom | WHILST THE GREAT OCEAN OF TRUTH LAY ALL UNDISCOVERED BEFORE ME. – ISAAC NEWTON |

## 3. THE BUTTERFLY (Chuang Tzu, tr. Herbert Giles, 1889)
- **Cast:** MR. CHEN (40s), white shirt, loosened blue tie with yellow dots, tired eyes.
  THE BUTTERFLY: blue wings with the tie's yellow dots.
- **Colour script:** grey-teal office at 3 a.m. → the dream in saturated tropical colour →
  pale dawn gold when he wakes.
- **Repetition:** the tie pattern: tie, then wings, then the shimmer on his fingertips.
- **Turn:** the wing dust on his fingers.

| # | shot | caption |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title. Wide: an empty open-plan office at night; one lit desk; clock 3:07 | ONCE UPON A TIME, I, CHUANG TZŬ, DREAMT I WAS A BUTTERFLY, |
| 2 | Close: Chen's head sinking onto his arms beside the keyboard | (none) |
| 3 | A butterfly lifts off his back; its wings carry his tie's pattern | FLUTTERING HITHER AND THITHER, TO ALL INTENTS AND PURPOSES A BUTTERFLY. |
| 4 | Wide: a tiny butterfly over the night city, out of the window | (none) |
| 5 | Dawn meadow on a rooftop garden: butterflies, flowers | I WAS CONSCIOUS ONLY OF FOLLOWING MY FANCIES AS A BUTTERFLY, |
| 6 | Close: the butterfly on a flower, sun behind | AND WAS UNCONSCIOUS OF MY INDIVIDUALITY AS A MAN. |
| 7 | BRRRING: the desk phone; Chen jolts up, papers flying | SUDDENLY, I AWAKED, AND THERE I LAY, MYSELF AGAIN. |
| 8 | Close: his fingertips glittering with blue and yellow dust | (none) |
| 9 | Splash: Chen at the open window at sunrise, a butterfly on his finger; they regard each other | NOW I DO NOT KNOW WHETHER I WAS THEN A MAN DREAMING I WAS A BUTTERFLY, OR WHETHER I AM NOW A BUTTERFLY DREAMING I AM A MAN. – CHUANG TZU |

## 4. SEVERAL THOUSAND THINGS (Thomas Edison, reported 1910)
- **Cast:** MAYA (11), curly hair, glasses, oversized apron. DAD, tired, tie loosened.
- **Colour script:** cheerful kitchen yellow, more flour-white every panel, then the golden loaf.
- **Repetition:** the fridge door filling up with photos of failed loaves.
- **Turn:** Dad reads the notes under the photos. It's not a wall of failures; it's a lab notebook.

| # | shot | words |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title. Kitchen: Maya with a handwritten card NANA'S LOAF, flour cloud | (none) |
| 2 | Loaf #1: a brick; she photographs it | (none) |
| 3–5 | Row of three: #6 burnt, #13 exploded, #22 raw; fridge filling | (none) |
| 6 | Dad in the doorway facing a fridge buried in photos | DAD: MAYA… THAT'S 47 FAILURES. |
| 7 | Maya, flour on her nose, grinning | RESULTS! WHY, MAN, I HAVE GOTTEN A LOT OF RESULTS! |
| 8 | Close: the notes under the photos: TOO MUCH SALT, OVEN TOO HOT, YEAST WAS DEAD… | I KNOW SEVERAL THOUSAND THINGS THAT WON'T WORK. |
| 9 | Splash: loaf #48, golden and steaming; Dad and Maya tearing it open; the fridge behind | – THOMAS EDISON |

## 5. WOBBLING WILL (Frances E. Willard, 1895)
Willard learned to ride a bicycle at 53 and wrote a book about it.

- **Cast:** PEG (53), retired school cook, grey bun, glasses, mustard cardigan, knee-length skirt.
  KIDS on scooters. A DUCK.
- **Colour script:** cold blue-grey early mornings of falling → warm orange the morning it works.
- **Repetition:** three identical park panels, three different falls; a growing tally in her notebook.
- **Turn:** she stops looking down at the wobbling front wheel and looks at the bridge far ahead.

| # | shot | words |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title. Peg wheels a second-hand bicycle out of the shed; price tag still on | (none) |
| 2 | She pushes off, wobble lines | (sfx) WOBBLE WOBBLE |
| 3 | CRASH into the hedge | CRASH! |
| 4–6 | Same park framing ×3: puddle; kids laughing; bandaged | (sfx) SPLOSH / HA HA |
| 7 | Close: her notebook: FALLS, with 23 tally marks | (none) |
| 8 | Dusk: she sits on the bench, bike on its side | I FINALLY CONCLUDED |
| 9 | Close: her eyes locked on the wobbling front wheel, drawn huge | THAT ALL FAILURE WAS FROM A WOBBLING WILL |
| 10 | She lifts her head to the bridge and the sunrise | RATHER THAN A WOBBLING WHEEL. |
| 11 | Splash: Peg flying along the river path, kids cheering, duck airborne | – FRANCES E. WILLARD, WHO LEARNED TO RIDE AT 53 |
